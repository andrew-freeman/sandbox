That's not a terrible idea — it's actually the same move Bennett made when he invented logical depth, and it's closer to already-existing prior art than the chirality one was. Let me actually build something rather than just react to it.

First, the honest problem with the framing, stated precisely: "computational difficulty" as a property of a trajectory is exactly what Kolmogorov complexity and logical depth already try to capture — which we flagged as candidates back in the first review. So "we live in a simulation" isn't adding new math; the math is the same complexity-measure question as before. What "simulation" adds is a *reason* something like that might matter — but it also forces a choice with no good answer built in: does the simulator favor computationally *cheap* trajectories (shortcuts, lower resolution when unobserved) or *expensive* ones (more resources spent where something interesting is happening)? Nothing in the setup picks a sign, same problem as chirality's undetermined ±1, except here there isn't even a known physical asymmetry (like the weak force) to hang it on.

There's a sharper, more concrete problem underneath that, though, and it's worth actually seeing rather than just stating: complexity measures are properties of *whole trajectories*, not single transitions. Every toy model so far tilted one step at a time — P_phys(y|a,b)·exp(ε·I(a,b,y)). Compressed length doesn't decompose that way; you can't meaningfully ask "how much does this one transition contribute to the whole sequence's compressibility." So this candidate can't reuse any of the existing machinery — it needs the *global* path-reweighting version of the hypothesis, the one I flagged a few turns back as needing the Doob-transform/driven-process treatment, not the local per-step shortcut everything else has used.

Let me actually try the global version and see what happens.Ran it on the same P1 process from a few turns back, using gzip-compressed length as the complexity proxy — a real, standard approximation to Kolmogorov complexity, the same one behind normalized-compression-distance — and importance-reweighting a sample of whole trajectories by exp(ε·complexity).

Two honest findings. First: compressed length is nearly uncorrelated with simple aggregate observables (r≈0.05 with fraction of time in state 1), but it does track structure — mean run length between symbol flips drops cleanly as ε increases, so positive ε favors less bursty, harder-to-predict trajectories. That's internally sensible: less patterned sequences really are less compressible.

Second, and more important: this degenerates fast, and predictably worse as trajectories get longer.

```
L=200   eps=0.1  ESS/n=0.83   eps=0.5  ESS/n=0.10   eps=1.0  ESS/n=0.01
L=2000  eps=0.1  ESS/n=0.46   eps=0.5  ESS/n=0.008  eps=1.0  ESS/n=0.002
```

At 200 steps, ε up to about 0.1–0.2 leaves a reasonably intact sample; by ε=1, one trajectory out of 4000 is carrying essentially all the weight, so whatever you'd report at that point is that single trajectory's properties, not an ensemble average. Stretch to 2000 steps — same ε values, ten times the trajectory — and the collapse happens far earlier: effective sample size is already down to under 1% by ε=0.5. That's not a fluke, it's forced: compressed length scales roughly with trajectory length, so tilting by it means tilting by an extensive quantity, and exp(ε·X) gets exponentially more sensitive to sampling noise as X grows. That's a real problem here specifically, because the statistical-power numbers from a few turns back said you might need 10⁵–10⁶-step trajectories to detect a small ε in the first place — and this says ε has to shrink as trajectories get longer just to keep the reweighting well-posed at all. Those two requirements pull in opposite directions, and nothing here reconciles them.

This is exactly the situation the driven-process/Doob-transform point from earlier was for. Sampling under P_phys and reweighting afterward, which is what I just did, is the crude way to implement a path reweighting, and it's exactly the approach that degenerates for extensive functionals. The standard fix — what population-dynamics/cloning algorithms in large-deviation theory are built for (Giardinà–Kurchan–Peliti is the usual reference) — is to derive the local transition rule that's actually equivalent to the global tilt and simulate *that* directly, instead of reweighting samples after they're generated. That was worth doing for the reparameterization question before; now there's a second, sharper, purely numerical reason to do it.

One more thing worth having on the table: the closest existing work that's actually cleared the falsifiability bar in "we live in a simulation" territory isn't about complexity-weighted probabilities — it's Beane, Davoudi & Savage, "Constraints on the Universe as a Numerical Simulation" (2012, *Eur. Phys. J. A* 2014), which assumes the universe is a lattice-QCD-style simulation and derives a specific, checkable prediction: an underlying grid would show up as a departure from perfect rotational symmetry in the highest-energy cosmic ray spectrum, giving an actual numerical bound on the lattice spacing from existing data (inverse spacing ≳10¹¹ GeV). Different mechanism entirely — spatial discreteness, not an I-as-complexity coupling — but it's the one place in this territory that did what your own falsifiability section demands: a derived, checkable number instead of an intuition. Worth reading as the shape a real pre-registered prediction here would need to take, even though the specific mechanism doesn't transfer.
